Sea Surface Temperature (SST)

    Sea surface temperature (SST) plays a key role in air-sea heat exchange, but infrared satellite observations are subject to observation gaps caused by clouds and noise, while numerical models are computationally intensive and take a long time to reflect real-time conditions. CARE-SST and PARAN are two deep learning techniques that fuse physical and background information with satellite observation data to reconstruct high-resolution SST distributions without observational gaps.

  • CARE-SST: a context-aware denoising diffusion model that fuses VIIRS infrared imagery with OISST background fields to produce global, daily, ~2 km gap-filled SST, recovering fine-scale currents and fronts even under heavy cloud cover.

  • PARAN: a Physics-Assisted Reconstruction Adversarial Network combining Himawari-8 geostationary data with physical drivers (solar radiation, wind speed) via GAN to generate continuous, 2 km, hourly SST over the Northwest Pacific (2019–2021), accurately capturing diurnal warming.

Sea Surface Salinity (SSS)

   Sea Surface Salinity (SSS) is a key parameter of the ocean, and it serves as a proxy for climate change by interacting directly with the atmosphere. We are monitoring and predicting SSS in the Northeast Asia Sea region based on artificial intelligence (AI) methods. We are using the fusion of multiple satellite, reanalysis models, and in-situ data. From our studies, the SSS of the Northeast Asia Sea can be monitored more accurately, and it can reduce damage from low salinity water by predicting SSS in summer.

  • Effective, novel gap-filling method for generating bias-corrected global daily SSS without delay

  • Using machine learning approaches, generate high resolution SSS from Geostationary Ocean Color Imager (GOCI) data

Coastal Water Quality

    Monitoring water quality over coastal areas is important as most industrial facilities and residential areas are located along the coast. There are several representative water quality parameters, such as chlorophyll-a, dissolved oxygen, and suspended solids. Field-measured water quality parameters do not provide spatial and temporal continuity, so satellite remote sensing can be an alternative for identifying surface water quality. We are monitoring coastal water quality around the Korean Peninsula based on artificial intelligence (AI) methods. From our studies, the water quality of South Korea can be monitored continuously, and hourly available Geostationary Ocean Color Imager (GOCI) images are useful to discuss spatiotemporal distributions of the water quality parameters.

  • Estimating water quality index (WQI) around the Korean Peninsula

  • Analyzing the Spatio-temporal variability of water quality

  • Developing a high-resolution Forel-Ule-Index (FUI) calculation algorithm for Korean waters using observational data and machine learning techniques

Harmful Algal Blooms

    The harmful algal blooms (HABs), or red tide, are one of the significant coastal disasters worldwide, which causes large socio-economic damages in the fishery and aquaculture industries. However, red tide water, which has high red and infra-red reflectance, often causes failure in atmospheric correction, making quantification algorithms based on remote sensing reflectance produce large errors. We are to develop a red tide detection algorithm using satellite data based on machine learning.

  • Red tide detection in the coastal area of the south sea of Korea

  • Analysis of the diurnal cycle of the red tide using satellites

  • Spatio-temporal analysis of HABs using satellites

Blue Carbon

   An acceleration of climate change in recent years has led to increased attention towards 'blue carbon' which refers to the carbon captured by the ocean. The monitoring of ocean carbon dioxide is important for a better understanding of the role of the ocean as a carbon sink, and regional and global carbon cycles. We estimated the surface seawater ƒCO2 in the East Sea of Korea using in situ measurements, GOCI satellite and their derived products, and HYCOM reanalysis data through machine learning approaches.

   To understand marine ecosystems, we classified and analyzed global marine eco-provinces using k-means clustering considering carbon cycling. The findings can also be employed in the development of guidelines for the systematic management of vulnerable coastal regions to climate change.

  • Estimation of the fugacity of carbon dioxide (fCO2) over the East Sea using in situ measurements, satellite data, products from the GOCI and HYCOM reanalysis data

  • Classification and analysis of global marine eco-provinces using k-means clustering, considering carbon cycling

Blue Carbon

   Blue carbon — carbon captured and stored by ocean ecosystems — plays a key role in mitigating climate change, and chlorophyll-a (Chl-a), the primary pigment of phytoplankton photosynthesis, serves as a core indicator of the ocean's carbon sequestration capacity. However, sparse in-situ observations and the ocean's complex physical dynamics have long made it difficult to reconstruct the global 3-D (vertical) distribution of Chl-a. Existing AI- and numerical-model-based approaches tend to underestimate or smooth out depth-dependent variability such as the subsurface chlorophyll maximum (SCM).

  • Hybrid ML framework combining satellite ocean color, 3-D physical reanalysis, and BGC-Argo profiles — PCM classifies Chl-a profile shapes and LGBM estimates structural parameters (SCM depth, width) — to reconstruct global 3-D Chl-a at 0.25° resolution (R² = 0.821, RMSE = 0.092 mg/m³).

  • By capturing vertical profile geometry, the model overcomes the smoothing/underestimation bias of prior neural network and numerical models, improving global ocean productivity and biological carbon pump estimates.

Ocean-fog

    Ocean-fog is a kind of cloud that attaches its base to the ocean surface, with a large number of droplets that lowers the visibility of less than 1km. Since 2013, about 60 accidents caused by low-visibility have occurred annually over the Korean ocean; thus, the detection and prediction of the ocean-fog are important to prevent socio-economic losses. We use a combination of station measurement, satellite products, and numerical weather prediction (NWP) model products in conjunction with deep-learning techniques to detect and predict ocean-fog.

  • For ocean–fog detection via the Himawari-8 satellite using machine-learning and an objective procedure, regardless of whether it is day or night

  • Advanced ocean fog prediction model for the Yellow Sea region, leveraging satellite-based detection and high-performance data-driven methods
