Methane estimation

    Methane (CH₄) is a potent greenhouse gas, responsible for nearly 30% of the global warming effect since the pre-industrial era. Due to its significant role in climate change and its shorter atmospheric lifetime, accurately monitoring methane levels is critical for effective climate action. Precise methane data helps researchers understand sources, sinks, and overall atmospheric behavior, enabling more targeted and effective mitigation strategies.

    In this study, we aim to improve global methane concentration (XCH₄) estimates by applying machine learning techniques. We use data from multiple satellite missions, including GOSAT, GOSAT-2, and Sentinel-5P, which provide important methane measurements but often contain biases due to differences in sensors, retrieval algorithms, and atmospheric conditions. To address these issues, we employ advanced machine learning approaches to integrate satellite data with ground-based observations from networks like TCCON. This allows us to correct biases and achieve more accurate methane concentration estimates on a global scale. The goal of our project is to deliver improved XCH₄ data that can provide a more accurate understanding of global methane distributions, ultimately contributing to better-informed climate policies and strategies for mitigating methane emissions.

Global High-Resolution Methane Plume Detection

  Methane is the second most significant anthropogenic greenhouse gas after carbon dioxide and a major contributor to near-term climate warming, making rapid emission reductions an effective climate mitigation strategy. Hyperspectral satellite observations enable detailed methane plume detection through characteristic absorption features, but the relative advantages and complementary roles of enhancement-based and radiance-based approaches for large-scale monitoring have not been fully established.

  In this study, we develop a dual-path deep learning framework that systematically compares enhancement-based and radiance-based methane plume detection using EMIT observations. The enhancement-based pathway provides accurate and stable plume segmentation, while the radiance-based pathway enables rapid detection directly from hyperspectral radiance without computationally intensive enhancement retrieval. We further evaluate the physical consistency of learned spatial and spectral features using explainable AI and assess architecture-level robustness with Tanager-1 observations. The framework is also applied to characterize the global distribution and major emission sectors of detected methane plumes. Together, these results support a scalable and sensor-agnostic framework for global methane monitoring.

Long-term Carbon Dioxide (XCO2) Estimation

  Increasing awareness of greenhouse gases (GHGs) associated with climate change has highlighted the importance of monitoring atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2), one of the most important GHGs. Industrial activities and fossil fuel combustion have driven a long-term increase in atmospheric CO2 concentrations, making continuous and accurate monitoring essential for understanding its environmental impacts. The column-averaged dry-air mole fraction of CO2 (XCO2) can be monitored using satellite observations, atmospheric reanalysis datasets, and ground-based observation networks, each of which has its own advantages and limitations. In recent years, satellite observations have become an important tool for monitoring atmospheric CO2. Nevertheless, satellite-based XCO2 still suffers from spatial data gaps, and its temporal coverage is always constrained by satellite launch dates (e.g., since 2014 for OCO-2).

  In this study, we focus on estimating spatial missing values in OCO-2 satellite observations to reconstruct a long-term, high-resolution global XCO2 dataset using an AI-based approach. Unlike previous studies that rely highly on reanalysis products, our framework leverages satellite observations and extends the reconstruction to approximately 11 years before the OCO-2 launch in 2014, improving spatial coverage and temporal continuity.
