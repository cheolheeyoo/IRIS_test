Drought Monitoring

      Drought is one of the most complex natural disasters, making it difficult to clearly define its onset, intensity, and scope. Based on the impacts it causes, droughts are generally classified into four types: meteorological, agricultural, hydrological, and socioeconomic.


        To represent drought information, we utilize satellite data, meteorological data, and other relevant sources to conduct large-scale drought monitoring. Through satellite observations, we monitor vegetation vitality, rainfall patterns, surface temperature, and other environmental factors. By analyzing this data, we quantify the stress caused by specific types of droughts (Park et al., 2017; Park et al., 2020, …).

   Furthermore, to provide comprehensive drought information, we are developing new drought monitoring methods and indices tailored to user needs (Son et al., 2021). This method considers multiple interdependent variables on a continental scale, allowing users to focus on the type of drought most relevant to their interests.

    Additionally, our research delves into the causes of drought and explores key indicators that represent drought phenomena, aiming to enhance understanding and response strategies.

Drought Forecasting

      Drought forecasts are critical for managing drought-related damage effectively and providing timely, relevant information to decision-makers. To address this, we are developing drought forecast models that blend the temporal patterns of drought indices with numerical model  forecast outputs using advanced deep learning techniques. These models are designed to provide forecasts on both short-term (~days) and (sub)seasonal time scales.

        Key aspects of our research include the development of week-to-seasonal drought forecast models, the application of artificial intelligence techniques, particularly deep learning, and the integration of remote sensing data with model outputs to enhance the accuracy and reliability of drought forecasts (Park et al., 2018; Park et al., 2020; In progress)

Soil Moisture Retrieval

      Soil Moisture (SM), the water content held in the spaces between soil particles, plays a critical role in maintaining ecosystems and agricultural productivity. One of the information sources of the SM is satellite remote sensing. Especially, microwave remote sensing provides and unique opportunity to retrieve SM from space.


        We trying to improve those SM data accuracy (Lee et al., 2022) and availability (Lee et al., 2024) by incorporating three main data sources—ground measurement, satellite measurement, and numerical models—with state-of-the-art deep-learning (DL) algorithms.

   Furthermore, we trying to understand how the DL model works—Generally regarded as a “black-box”—by inversely tracking DL models with physical equations (Lee at al., 2024; TGRS).

    We anticipate that our study results can improve our understanding of global water and carbon cycles.
