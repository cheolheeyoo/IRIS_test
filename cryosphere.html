Deep learning-based Arctic melt pond detection

       Arctic melt ponds play a significant role in the reduction of sea ice extent and changes in the Arctic climate, and their accurate monitoring is important. However, their observation methods may suffer from reduced accuracy under varying weather conditions. To address this, different approaches capable of providing high-resolution data are needed. ICESat-2 measures surface elevation with high precision, enabling the detection of smaller melt ponds that were previously undetectable by CryoSat-2. Altimetry data can be utilized for polar research regardless of weather conditions, making it a valuable tool for studying the Arctic cryosphere.


        In this study, we developed a method to detect Arctic summer melt ponds using ICESat-2 altimetry data. For each ICESat-2 point, we analyzed the standard deviation of elevation and the number of photons within each segment to distinguish between sea ice and melt ponds. Since melt ponds have smoother surfaces than sea ice, they exhibit lower elevation variation and different photon counts, which were used to classify melt ponds covered by water and those covered by ice. The proposed detection method was validated using Sentinel-2 optical imagery and effectively identified various types of melt ponds.

Deep learning-based Arctic SIC prediction

       Accurate prediction of Arctic sea-ice concentration (SIC) is essential for maritime planning and climate monitoring, yet traditional numerical and statistical models often struggle—especially during rapid summer ice decline. Recent deep-learning approaches offer a promising alternative by better capturing nonlinear relationships and spatiotemporal patterns in SIC variability.


        Kim et al. (2020) developed a CNN-based monthly SIC prediction model and showed that it outperformed a Random Forest model and a monthly trend baseline, with particularly strong skill during extreme rapid-decline cases.

    Kim et al. (2025) further advanced this direction by proposing a U-Net-based pan-Arctic monthly SIC forecasting framework for 3–12-month lead times, demonstrating consistently stable performance against operational forecasts (C3S), damped anomaly persistence, and other deep-learning baselines. Their analysis also indicated that antecedent SIC and surface temperature are key contributors to long-lead predictability, while solar radiation and winds can be more influential in thin-ice regions.

Quantification of sea ice characteristics

      Sea ice indices such as sea ice concentration (SIC) play a key role in monitoring climate change. However, it does not fully capture the vulnerability of sea ice to melting, especially under conditions of floe fragmentation. To address this limitation, we introduce a novel metric—the floe fragmentation index (FFI)—designed to quantify the degree of fragmentation of sea ice. FFI increases as sea ice becomes more fragmented. Even under identical SIC conditions (e.g., 50%), the reference FFI captured distinct differences in floe structure, demonstrating its ability to represent fragmentation more explicitly than SIC.

        At the point of sea ice disappearance within this area, no early signs were observed in SIC, whereas FFI did reveal such signals. In particular, under conditions where the sea ice was highly fragmented and thus more likely to drift away or melt, SIC remained close to 100%, while FFI captured the relatively severe fragmentation of the sea ice. The FFI complemented SIC by capturing fragmentation changes, potentially allowing earlier detection of SIC change in the melting seasons.

Deep learning-based Antarctic SIC prediction

      Antarctic sea-ice concentration has declined sharply since 2015, and robust long-term prediction is required to determine whether this change is a temporary fluctuation or a persistent shift. Traditionally, Antarctic SIC prediction has relied on statistical methods and physics-based numerical models. Recently, deep-learning approaches have emerged as computationally efficient alternatives with competitive performance. However, many existing deep-learning studies focus on a single climate variable, which limits their ability to represent the complex ocean–atmosphere interactions and sea-ice dynamics that govern Antarctic sea-ice variability.

        To address this gap, this study proposes a deep-learning–based long-term forecasting framework that integrates diverse oceanic and atmospheric predictors. We employ a spatiotemporal forecasting model well suited for time-series imagery and further enhance spatial details in a post-processing stage by applying residual correction to refine fine-scale structures and ice-edge features.
