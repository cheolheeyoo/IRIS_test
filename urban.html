Air Temperature

    Accurate temperature monitoring is critical to understanding and managing urban climate dynamics, especially in the context of rising global temperatures and increasing frequency of extreme events. However, due to the limited number of in situ air temperature monitoring stations, a significant gap exists in understanding detailed temperature patterns across cities.

      To address this problem, we utilize satellite-derived land surface temperature (LST) data, which provides comprehensive spatial coverage across urban terrain. We integrate machine learning techniques with LST data to reconstruct daily maximum and minimum temperatures for areas that cannot be directly measured.


      We also apply spatial interpolation methods to estimate air temperatures in unmonitored regions. We trained a machine learning model with satellite data and in situ observations to create a seamless spatial map of air temperature. Compared to the smoothed spatial distribution of the traditional geostatistical model, Cokriging, our model was found to better represent heterogeneous spatial patterns. This methodology enhances our ability to effectively monitor air temperature, helping us better understand and manage heat risks in cities.


 

Land Surface Temperature

     Land surface temperature (LST) is a crucial variable in understanding the thermal dynamics of the Earth's surface. It directly influences surface heat fluxes, energy balance, and plays a significant role in shaping the local climate, especially in urban environments. Despite its importance, continuous and seamless monitoring of LST is challenging due to the data gaps in satellite-based LST measurements interrupted by cloud cover and the limited number of in-situ observation stations.


    To fill the incomplete LST measurements under cloudy conditions, first, we corrected biases between satellite-derived LST and in-situ measurements to minimize errors caused by cloud-induced cold or warm biases. Furthermore, we incorporated binary cloud cover data into the model, enabling us to better account for cloud-related impacts on LST and improve the overall reliability of our temperature estimates.


    Our research also explores innovative methods for hourly LST estimation using a geostationary satellite data from GEO-KOMPSAT-2A (GK2A). By leveraging accumulated longwave and shortwave radiation data from GK2A, we are able to capture the historical influence of cloud effects on surface temperatures. This allows us to estimate LST with higher precision and temporal granularity, providing valuable insights for real-time heat monitoring and urban climate management.

 

LST Spatial Downscaling

     Satellite-based Land Surface Temperature (LST) is a major parameter for analyzing urban thermal environments. However, due to the heterogeneity of urban spaces, there is a growing demand for high-resolution LST products to enable more detailed analyses. To address this need, we aim to develop a machine learning (ML)-based model for high-resolution LST estimation, referred to as LST spatial downscaling.


    We utilized various satellite data with a Random Forest (RF) model to downscale MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) 1 km to 250 m. We demonstrated how high-resolution LST can reveal intra-urban LST variations based on land cover types. Additionally, we published a review paper that synthesized recent trends, limitations, and future research directions, expanding our understanding of LST spatial downscaling.


    RF is the most widely applied algorithm in LST downscaling, demonstrating robust performance. However, we noted the attenuation of extreme values and, in response to this issue, were the to apply Local Linear Forest (LLF) for spatial enhancement, demonstrating its efficacy.



 

Short-term Heatwave Forecasting

     Forecasts of maximum and minimum air temperatures are essential to mitigate the damage of extreme weather events such as heat waves and tropical nights. We aim to develop more accurate air temperature prediction models by correcting the prediction bias of numerical weather prediction models using various machine learning (ML) and deep-learning (DL) based post‐processing techniques. Two numerical models provided by the Korean Meteorological Administration, LDAPS and GDAPS, are the targets.


    First, we developed three ML-based and one ensemble bias correction models for Seoul. The original LDAPS showed an R2 of 0.69 and a bias of -0.85℃ in the next day’s maximum temperature forecast. Our ensemble model showed improved forecast skill with an R2 of 0.78 and a bias of -0.13℃. Second, we applied DL to the bias correction model and expanded the study area to South Korea. Additionally, we developed a novel model that combines spatial downscaling and bias correction using a DL technique. In particular, we successfully predicted the daily minimum and maximum air temperature for 7 days across South Korea compared to GDAPS.




 

Long-term Heatwave Forecasting

     With climate change and frequent extreme weather events, long-term predictions of heat waves are becoming increasingly important for risk mitigation and disaster preparedness. We focused on distant ocean and land variability and their potential to influence South Korea's summer climate. This study identified global climate variables that are important for predicting heat waves in South Korea.


    We examined four climate components: soil moisture, snow depth, sea surface temperature, and sea ice concentration. Through long-term nonlinear correlation analysis, we selected a total of 16 winter or spring climate variabilities. Then, we built a machine learning-based heat wave prediction model using these variables as independent variables and the number of heat waves as the dependent variable. The model performed best when compared to one simple statistical model and one physics-based climate model. We evaluated the most important teleconnection drivers through explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) analysis and further analyzed their association with South Korea’s summer climate patterns and large-scale teleconnections which has already been demonstrated to be associated with the favorable condition for heatwaves in South Korea.





 

Local Climate Zone

     The Local Climate Zone (LCZ) scheme is a standardized classification system that represents the characteristics of urban forms and functions, particularly in urban heat island (UHI) research. It defines 17 distinct zones based on features such as building and tree height, density, and surface cover types.


    We are the first to apply convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to LCZ mapping. Previous work has utilized satellite imagery and random forest (RF) classifiers for classification, but we significantly improved accuracy performance by using image-based deep learning technology.


    In addition, we proposed a method to improve the difficulty of classifying complex urban structures using satellite images alone by using geographic information system (GIS)-based building data. In particular, our framework selectively choose LCZ classification results based on the classification certainty of deep learning classifiers to reflect the limitations of incomplete building information.


    Our team remains committed to applying the LCZ framework to diverse studies, advancing sustainable urban development and environmental stewardship.

Urban Thermal Environment Analysis

     As cities continue to grow and urbanize rapidly, the need to effectively monitor and manage the urban thermal environment becomes increasingly critical. The urban heat island (UHI) effect, combined with the impacts of climate change, presents significant challenges to public health, energy consumption, and urban sustainability. Understanding how urban structures and population dynamics contribute to heat distribution is essential for developing resilient cities.


    To address these challenges, we conducted a study in Suwon, South Korea, by comparing urban structure changes between 2004 and 2021 and its impact on LST. By employing LST downscaling techniques, we generated high-resolution (30 m) LST maps, which allowed us to closely examine thermal changes. We then analyzed the impact of urban expansion—both horizontal and vertical—on the thermal environment, providing valuable insights into how urban growth influences heat distribution across the city.


    We proposed a diurnal heat risk assessment framework that integrated spatiotemporal air temperature and real-time population data. Using machine learning techniques, we produced high-resolution (250-meter) daily Tmax and Tmin maps, which better matched the actual heat exposure of the population and effectively showed the diurnal variation of heat risk between day and night.

Urban Carbon Emission

     Quantification of carbon emissions often relies on a top-down approach that multiplies annual national fuel consumption by an emissions factor. However, this method typically results in low spatial and temporal resolution. Carbon emissions are also observed directly, through stack emissions measurements or in-situ monitoring stations. In addition, advancements in satellite technology are rapidly enhancing global carbon emission monitoring capabilities.


    Urban areas are significant sources of carbon emissions due to various human activities. With increasing urbanization, there is a growing demand for high-resolution spatio-temporal technology to estimate carbon emissions. Particularly, emissions from road transportation, driven by rising traffic, have become one of the largest direct sources of carbon emissions. In this study, we aim to estimate carbon emissions by integrating traffic volume data with artificial intelligence. We will used a deep learning model to construct traffic volume in a city and calculate vehicle emissions on this data.
