Carbon Flux

    Global warming and climate change have significantly altered vegetation ecosystems, affecting how plants perform photosynthesis. Monitoring these biological processes is crucial for understanding the Earth’s carbon cycle and informing climate policies, yet accurately tracking them remains challenging due to the large spatiotemporal variability in vegetation responses to external factors. This study hypothesizes that incorporating environmental factors can effectively represent changes in carbon exchange processes.


  In this research, we use machine learning and deep learning models to estimate gross primary productivity (GPP), ecosystem respiration (Reco), and net ecosystem exchange (NEE) based on various input features, analyzing which variables most impact plant carbon exchange. We are conducting two separate studies utilizing data from polar-orbiting and geostationary satellites to leverage their respective spatial and temporal advantages in monitoring terrestrial carbon flux. This approach demonstrates the potential of satellite observations combined with environmental variables to analyze how external factors influence GPP variations.

Carbon Storage

    Vegetation carbon storage is crucial in mitigating climate change by capturing atmospheric carbon dioxide in plant biomass. Monitoring carbon stock is essential for understanding how vegetation contributes to the global carbon cycle and ecosystem health. Satellite data enables efficient, large-scale tracking of vegetation and carbon dynamics, providing accurate assessments of carbon sequestration potential to inform climate policies.

  We have specifically used high-resolution satellite imagery to assess vegetation biomass and carbon storages not only in forests but also in urban areas. Urban vegetation, although often fragmented and heterogeneous, plays an important role in improving environmental quality and enhancing carbon sequestration within cities. Achieving carbon neutrality requires sustainable green infrastructure in urban environments, as well as continuous monitoring to support effective management and planning. Using airborne Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR), satellite multispectral imagery, and Geographic Information System (GIS) data, we applied AI techniques to estimate urban vegetation carbon stock (Lee et al., 2024).

Vegetation monitoring

    Satellite remote sensing provides a powerful approach for vegetation monitoring by exploiting the spectral characteristics of vegetation. These spectral signals enable continuous monitoring of vegetation condition over large areas, which is essential for understanding dynamic changes in vegetation health. In our research, we monitor vegetation conditions using various vegetation indices derived from satellite imagery, including widely used indicators such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and Leaf Area Index (LAI). In particular, LAI was corrected using the woody-to-total-area ratio (α) derived from satellite reflectance, thereby compensating for the overestimation problem in field observations (Kim et al., 2025).


   Understanding land surface structure is also important for characterizing vegetation distribution. In this context, we address problems such as land cover classification to better represent surface characteristics. We also develop methods to extract detailed vegetation information in urban environments that is not typically provided in conventional land cover products, including urban tree canopy cover segmentation (Son et al., 2021), individual tree detection (Lee et al., 2023), and fractional tree canopy cover (FTC) maps (Bae et al., 2024).
