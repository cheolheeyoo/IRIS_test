Fire Risk Assessment

    Wildfires can cause extensive damage, including deforestation and environmental pollution, even from a single event. Restoring affected areas requires substantial time and resources. Therefore, assessing wildfire risk in advance is essential for effective preparedness and response. Most existing wildfire risk indices primarily rely on weather variables associated with ignition, while comprehensive analyses incorporating diverse environmental factors remain limited.


        Kang et al. (2019, 2020) addressed this limitation by developing an hourly forest fire risk index (HFRI) at 1 km resolution for South Korea. The index integrates accessibility, fuel, temporal, and meteorological factors using the CatBoost machine learning algorithm. HFRI was constructed from an ensemble framework combining integrated and meteorological models, enabling more spatially detailed and seasonally stable simulations compared to earlier indices. Feature contributions analyzed using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) revealed that relative humidity, elevation, road density, and population density were key drivers, underscoring the critical role of accessibility in human-induced wildfire risk.
 

Fire Weather Forecasting

    To minimize the damage caused by forest fires, accurate risk forecasting is essential. Currently, South Korea’s forest fire risk forecasting system provides only short-term predictions, limited to 48 hours. However, the increasing frequency of both small- and large-scale forest fires has resulted in substantial damage, rendering short-term forecasts insufficient for effective response. This highlights the need for mid-range forecasting to enable more proactive wildfire management.


    Park et al. (2022) developed 7-day mid-range forest fire risk prediction models for South Korea using a random forest algorithm. The models incorporated weather forecasts, historical and current Fire Risk Index (FRI), elevation, forest fire hazard ratings, and drought indices as input variables. The results demonstrated high predictive accuracy and strong agreement with observed wildfire occurrences. Notably, incorporating historical FRI values yielded more accurate predictions than using FRI trends, regardless of the drought index applied.

Active Fire Detection

    To minimize wildfire damage, rapid and coordinated responses enabled by early detection and real-time monitoring are essential. Geostationary satellite imagery, with its high temporal resolution, facilitates near-real-time detection over large areas. However, most existing wildfire detection algorithms rely on threshold-based approaches applied to satellite imagery, limiting their spatiotemporal generalizability. Our goal is to develop a robust real-time wildfire monitoring system that operates reliably across diverse environmental conditions by integrating AI-based models with satellite imagery, weather data, and observational variables.


     Following the work of Jang et al. (2019), who reduced false alarms through machine learning-based post-processing, numerous studies have incorporated AI techniques into wildfire detection. Kang et al. (2022) emphasized the importance of spatial information by comparing random forest and convolutional neural networks, while Lee et al. (2023) proposed efficient deep learning approaches through systematic analysis of state-of-the-art architectures. More recently, Kang et al. (2023) introduced a dual-module framework to independently process satellite imagery and auxiliary variables, and Sung et al. (2024, 2025) applied techniques to remove environmental trends from satellite observations to enhance detection robustness.

Fire Radiative Power Estimation

      Satellite remote sensing provides essential information on fire radiative power (FRP), a key variable for estimating biomass burning emissions and understanding fire dynamics. However, FRP evaluation remains challenging due to limited field validation data and differences in sensor characteristics across satellite platforms. Previous intercomparison studies have attempted to assess FRP uncertainty, but they are often limited in scope and influenced by differences in fire detection capabilities.

        To address these limitations, Kang et al. (2026) conducts a global evaluation of FRP from three satellite sensors—MODIS, VIIRS, and SLSTR—by harmonizing fire clusters while preserving sensor-specific detection characteristics. In addition, extended triple collocation (ETC) analysis is applied to assess FRP consistency without relying on reference data. The results highlight overall agreement among sensors, with regional differences reflecting variations in fire characteristics and detection sensitivity.

Burn Scar Analysis

    Burn scar analysis is essential not only for delineating damaged areas but also for guiding forest and ecosystem restoration, preventing secondary hazards such as landslides, and providing critical data for climate studies. Wildfire damage assessment can be conducted using either optical or synthetic aperture radar (SAR) imagery. Although optical imagery generally provides high accuracy, its performance is often hindered by cloud and smoke interference, reducing timeliness and reliability. In contrast, SAR enables all-weather data acquisition; however, it is subject to higher uncertainty due to inherent speckle noise, necessitating additional preprocessing and advanced AI-based techniques.


    Sim et al. (2020) developed a machine learning model integrating Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 data to estimate wildfire severity. Lee et al. (2021) utilized Sentinel-1 SAR data combined with principal component analysis (PCA) and K-means clustering to estimate burned areas, achieving high accuracy when validated against local reference data. More recently, Rana et al. (2026) evaluated five U-Net variants for Sentinel-1 SAR-based burned area mapping. By analyzing their performance under different environmental and observational conditions, these studies identify conditions in which specific components underperform and provides insights into strategies for overcoming these limitations.

Emission Estimation

      Wildfires emit substantial amounts of air pollutants and greenhouse gases, degrading local air quality and contributing to global climate change. Emission estimates derived from satellite-based fire radiative energy provide essential baseline data for air quality assessment and climate research.

    Kang et al. (2024) and Kang and Im (2024) utilized geostationary satellite imagery to address the underestimation issues in existing emission products and investigated the impacts of wildfire emissions on surrounding air quality.
